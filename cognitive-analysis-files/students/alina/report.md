# Cognitive Task Analysis: Alina Restrepo (Grade 9) — School Year 2025–2026

## Snapshot

Across 17 sessions and eight months, Alina's transcripts show a student whose language barrier and content ability are two separate variables that she is unusually good at telling apart. Again and again she names, precisely, which part of a task was English and which part was the actual subject — "the whole problem I could do, the word I did not know was the only part I was stuck on" — and that self-diagnosis is itself real, frequent metacognitive work, not just a study habit. Her strongest content thinking shows up in math and science, where she drives her own reasoning and asks the AI only to confirm it, and by spring the same independence starts appearing in history and literature as her English catches up. The single most important thing to know: her best original thinking — in a Critical Thinking session comparing two biased historical sources, in a Creative session drafting an artist's statement — happens when she's given room to reason or generate in whichever language gets her there fastest, and both peaks arrived only in the second half of the year, once she trusted the tool enough to think out loud in it.

**Evidence base:** 65 student turns across 17 sessions spanning September 2025 to May 2026, across five subject areas. This is enough breadth and duration to support a genuine profile, including a real change over the year.

## What the evidence shows

### Metacognitive — telling language apart from content, over and over

This is the most frequent type in the transcript, and it's unusually specific. In a geometry word problem, she opened by distinguishing exactly what was blocking her: "Entiendo el problema pero no entiendo la pregunta" (T15, sess_alina_03) — she understood the problem, not the question, and said so precisely rather than reporting general confusion. She solved the entire problem herself once that one phrase was clarified, then closed the session naming the distinction directly: "so the whole problem i could do, the word i did not know was the only part i was stuck on" (T21).

The same move recurs with her own writing. Fixing a recurring grammar error in an English essay, she asked for the general rule behind it rather than just the fix: "i always forget the "to" part. is there a rule for when i need to?" (T33, sess_alina_05). And by May, the metacognition turns real-time: rehearsing a claim she planned to say out loud in class for the first time all year, she noticed the gap between her written and spoken English as it happened — "that is what i want to say but it comes out slower when i say it than when i write it" (T105, sess_alina_16) — then practiced until it closed: "let me try it two more times... ok that one was faster" (T107).

### Conceptual — repairing her own models, especially in science and math

Her clearest strength shows up when a new fact conflicts with what she already believed. In a biology session on mitosis, a grammar check on her own sentence — "The cell divide in two identical parts." (T73, sess_alina_12) — surfaced a real gap in her model: "wait, i thought mitosis makes two identical cells, that is what the diagram showed. are they not identical?" (T75). She then repaired the model herself, using her own diagram as evidence: "the diagram shows the chromosomes split evenly to each side before the cell divides, so the dna is the same, but you are saying the cell parts around it might not split evenly. i did not think about that part before" (T77) — a textbook example of a model appearing and getting fixed in the transcript itself.

The same pattern, at full strength, appears in an osmosis lab write-up, where she stated her complete mechanistic model unprompted before asking about only the one part she wasn't sure of: "the potato in salt water lost mass because the water inside the potato moved outside where there was more salt, to make it more even... is this correct or am i missing something about why it moves that direction" (T38, sess_alina_06).

### Critical Thinking — a self-designed method for weighing biased sources

Notably absent from the first half of the year and fully realized by April. Given two conflicting accounts of the same protest — a British officer's report and a colonial newspaper — she was asked to decide which to trust. Rather than picking a side, she reasoned out each source's motive to distort the same event in opposite directions entirely on her own: "the british officer works for the government that is trying to stop the protests, so he would want to make it look small... the newspaper is colonial so they would want to make it look big and peaceful" (T89, sess_alina_14). Asked how to actually use that insight, she designed her own method rather than asking the AI for one: "i think you can still find the part where they agree even though they disagree on numbers, and that part is probably true because neither of them had a reason to lie about it. i need to reread both and find what they actually agree on instead of just picking the one i believe more" (T91) — a genuinely historian-grade move, and the strongest single turn in the corpus.

A smaller instance of the same instinct appears two months earlier, building a comparative argument about the causes of the American Revolution: "i think representation matters more than taxation because people were more angry about not having a voice than about the money itself" (T54, sess_alina_09).

### Creative — needs Spanish-first thinking to reach its peak

Rare in this corpus, and the one clear instance is worth a close look. Asked to write an artist's statement in English, she said the translation step was flattening her own idea: "i have the ideas in spanish in my head but writing it in english makes it sound simple and boring" (T94, sess_alina_15). Invited to state the idea in Spanish first, she produced a fully original, well-developed metaphor: a painting in two halves, one side in colors she knows from home, the other in colors she doesn't have the English words for yet (T96). She then directed the English draft's structure herself rather than accepting it as given, asking to lead with the idea instead of the description (T98). The lesson isn't that she lacks creative ideas — it's that English-first composition may be costing her access to her best ones.

### Transfer — rare, but real and self-initiated

In the last month of school, reviewing proofs for her geometry final, she noticed a structural resemblance to a history paragraph she'd written back in January and checked her own perception before trusting it: "tell me if this is a real connection or if i am just imagining it. in a geometry proof each step has to follow from the step before with a reason, or the whole proof falls apart. that is the same structure as the history paragraph i wrote in january" (T110, sess_alina_17). She then extended the connection further, mapping a specific past math mistake onto the identical pattern in a specific past history mistake: "the same mistake felt the same both times, when i skipped explaining why in the similarity proof back in february... that is the exact same problem as stating an opinion without evidence in history" (T112). One session in eighteen is a small sample, but it's self-initiated and fully sustained across three turns — a proven capability, not a lucky remark.

### Fluency and Exploratory — present, mostly shallow, and expected

Fluency is frequent but almost always Surface by design — vocabulary and terminology she already understands conceptually and needs the English word for. The one change worth noting: by January she starts phrasing these requests in English first rather than Spanish first ("i am trying to only ask in english now instead of spanish first," T52, sess_alina_08), a small but real behavioral shift. Exploratory work never lingers; each instance (a half-remembered literary term, a confused metaphor/simile distinction) resolves into real Conceptual or Critical Thinking work within a turn or two rather than staying pure intake.

## Where thinking was offloaded

Only one real instance in the whole corpus. Assigned four pages of dense U.S. History reading with one evening to finish it, she asked for the paragraph translated: "puedes traducir el primer parrafo completo para que pueda empezar" (T22, sess_alina_04). The tutor redirected her to read the sentence herself first, and she did — narrowing to the single word she didn't know ("established") and learning it (T24). Only then did she ask for the rest of the reading translated, explicitly naming the trade she was making: "ya aprendi la palabra que necesitaba" — she'd already gotten what she needed, and named the offload as a time-pressure decision rather than an attempt to skip the reading, agreeing to try highlighting new words herself next time (T26). It's the only offload in seventeen sessions, and it reads as a reasonable trade under a real deadline rather than a pattern worth flagging.

## What's missing

Creative and Transfer are the two thinnest categories, and both are thin for the same underlying reason: they show up only once each, but at full strength, which suggests the gap is opportunity rather than capability. Transfer needed nothing more than the right two subjects landing close enough together in her own mind; Creative needed permission to think in her first language before switching to her second. Neither looks like a skill she lacks — both look like conditions that haven't been offered often enough.

Exploratory staying shallow is not a concern in the same way: it's supposed to be a brief on-ramp, and in every instance here it correctly turns into deeper work within a turn or two rather than stalling in intake.

## For the teacher

**Give her a written channel for the source-evaluation work she's already doing.** Her strongest Critical Thinking (the primary-source comparison) is fully independent and sophisticated, but it surfaced in a tutoring session, not in Mr. Boyd's class, where she has reportedly been silent since September. A short written "which source, and why" option alongside class discussion would let this strength register somewhere a grade can see it.

**Prompt cross-subject connections on purpose, roughly monthly.** She made a genuine, sustained transfer between geometry and history entirely unprompted — but only once, in May. Ask her directly to connect what she's currently learning to another class; the capability is proven, it just hasn't had many occasions to run.

**Let her think or draft in her first language before switching to English, especially for open-ended writing.** Her one fully original piece of creative work only appeared once she was invited to think in Spanish first. Requiring English-first composition in other subjects may be quietly cutting her off from her best ideas, not just costing her time.

**Front-load reading support before dense homework lands with a same-night deadline.** The one offload in the corpus happened because four pages of unfamiliar vocabulary arrived with one evening to read them, not because she avoids the work — she has consistently shown she'll do the vocabulary work herself given the time.

## Caveats

This is one tool on one platform across one school year — it doesn't capture her in-class spoken participation, paper homework, or conversations with teachers and peers, which make up most of her actual academic life, and it can't confirm or explain Mr. Boyd's observation that she has been effectively silent in his class since September. The sessions are self-selected: she brought these conversations to the tutor, which skews the sample toward homework she was actively working through rather than everything she does independently without help. Code-switching between Spanish and English was read for content, not scored as an error — a monolingual read of this transcript risks conflating her language gaps with content gaps, which is exactly the distinction she herself draws all year. Depth ratings reflect what she wrote or said in the moment, not independently verified retention.
