# Cognitive Task Analysis: Student sam (Grade 11) — School Year 2025–2026

## Snapshot

Sam transferred into this school on December 1st, mid-year, after four years on a robotics team he'd built his identity around. His transcript splits cleanly into three phases: confident and technical at his old school (September–November), thin and withdrawn through the transfer disruption (December–February), and genuinely recovering from March onward. What holds the whole corpus together is an unusually honest metacognitive thread — he tracks his own decline in real time, and in one February session makes the connection between the move and his grades himself, out loud, seemingly for the first time. His clearest intellectual strength is conceptual repair: turning a memorized phrase or a half-right assumption into an actual working model, most vividly with orbital mechanics in March. The single most important thing to know: the recovery is real and it is legible in the transcript, and it tracks almost exactly with physics and robotics content specifically — that's not a coincidence worth losing.

**Evidence base:** 77 student turns across 17 sessions spanning September 2025 to May 2026, across five subjects, with a mid-year school transfer in the middle of the window. That is enough to see a real before-and-after, not just a snapshot.

Metacognitive and Conceptual both show real Substantive peaks sitting on top of a Developing typical — the capability is proven repeatedly, it just isn't uniform across the year, which is exactly what you'd expect from a student mid-disruption.

## What the evidence shows

### Metacognitive — the throughline of the whole year

This is the most frequent type in the transcript by a wide margin, and it's where the real story lives. From the first authored session he's already distinguishing a right answer from an understood one: after matching the answer key on a momentum problem, he says "ok so i think i got it right for the right reason then, i just didnt say the reason to myself while doing it" (T7, sess_7a1c9e02) — a habit of checking his own reasoning that predates the transfer entirely.

The turn that matters most in the whole corpus comes during his lowest point, five weeks after a 64 on a semester exam where he'd left two sections blank. Asked whether something other than physics might be going on, he answers: "i mean i moved here in december. and left my team and basically my whole life there. i guess thats not nothing. i didnt really think about it like a thing that would show up on a test though" (T82, sess_6f188e2c). He isn't being told this — he's arriving at it, in the moment, for what reads like the first time.

That same honesty shows up again in May, when he finally raises the idea of starting a robotics club: "i havent asked because i wasnt sure i wanted to get attached to anything here for a while, but its almost the end of the year and i actually miss building things with people" (T122, sess_a109de62) — naming his own avoidance and correctly noticing it has an expiration date. And in his final-exam planning session, he builds a study plan directly from a diagnosed past failure rather than a generic resolution: "go unit by unit starting with the ones i did worst on... and do practice problems instead of just rereading notes, since rereading is what i did in december and it didnt work at all" (T135, sess_ff3c60a8).

### Conceptual — building and repairing mental models, physics most often

His clearest single strength. In a physics session prepping for a unit test on circular motion, he opens by admitting a phrase he's memorized rather than understood — "it never fully clicked, i just memorized the phrase" — and then rebuilds the actual mechanism turn by turn: "its moving so fast sideways that by the time gravity pulls it down a bit, the curve of the earth has already dropped away underneath it by about the same amount... thats actually a genuinely different way of thinking about it than i had before, i was picturing gravity as basically turned off in orbit which is wrong" (T94, sess_e552a940).

The same move shows up in his lowest-engagement subject, during his lowest month. Asked to connect several causes of the Depression, he doesn't just list them — he builds the actual chain: "crash first, then people panicked and pulled money out of banks which caused the bank failures, and banks failing meant less credit for businesses which is part of why production and spending both dropped" (T68, sess_b31f6a90), then declines the offer of a written answer: "i can write that up myself, i just needed to see if i had the order right."

### Procedural — reliable, and honest about his own work

Sam consistently shows his own work before asking for a check, rather than asking for the work itself. In an October derivation session, he walks through a full momentum calculation and only afterward reveals why he distrusted it: "i got it wrong on the practice test last week from exactly that mistake, forgetting a direction sign, so now im double checking every one" (T20, sess_44b0f1d7).

His strongest Procedural evidence is also his most concrete: building a motor-control fix for his old robotics team and testing it against real hardware. He designs the test order himself, predicts the gap between the math and the real motor before seeing it, then reports back an actual measurement: "just P alone first, tune the gain until it settles close enough... the math version and the real motor probably wont agree" followed by "ok tried P alone with gain 0.4, it settles but leaves about a 3 degree gap consistently. going to add a small I term now" (T47, sess_5f0e8b2c).

### Transfer — rare, but consequential every time

Two of Sam's three real Transfer instances turn a physics concept into an actual decision rather than a new set of numbers. Explaining rotational inertia to a teammate, he reasons his own way to a design conclusion: "it needs way more torque because inertia scales with distance squared, not just distance. so a stronger motor helps some but if we couldve moved mass closer to the pivot that wouldve mattered more than the motor upgrade" (T38, sess_1e77d3aa) — a real engineering tradeoff, self-derived, with a consequence for his old team's robot.

The third instance closes the whole corpus: asked whether anything about this disrupted year is worth keeping for a hypothetical future move, he answers "probably talk to a teacher earlier instead of waiting five months to ask about a robotics club. i sat on that for no real reason" (T137, sess_ff3c60a8) — generalizing a specific lesson from this year into a portable rule.

### Critical Thinking and Creative — real, but confined to a handful of sessions

Both types are genuinely strong when they appear and largely absent elsewhere. In an essay revision, he identifies the actual distinction between two characters he's comparing rather than just asserting a comparison exists: "both characters refuse help right when its offered, but one does it because hes proud and the other does it because he doesnt trust the person offering" (T117, sess_3fe08a17) — and immediately diagnoses why the distinction didn't make it into his first draft: "i think i was just trying to get it done instead of actually caring about it" (T119).

Creative work is thinnest of all, essentially confined to one strong Fairbrook-era session where he generated three of his own interpretive framings for an essay and synthesized them into an arguable thesis before the AI did anything but ask him to sort them (T23–T25, sess_9c22ab41).

### Fluency and Exploratory — present, mostly shallow, and mostly during the low months

Fluency shows up as quick, correct recall — never the point of a session, which is appropriate. Exploratory is mostly thin and often abandoned mid-orientation, especially December through February, where two separate sessions end in the student simply walking away rather than engaging ("nvm ill just deal with it tomorrow," T61; "its fine ill just ask her tomorrow," T87). The one clear exception is the robotics-club question in May, which he follows all the way from "does this exist" to naming a specific teacher and a specific plan.

## Where thinking was offloaded

Genuine completed offloading is rare in this transcript, and what looks like it more often turns out to be avoidance of a subject or a person rather than avoidance of thinking. In a Computer Science session, asked to pair up for a project, he asks instead to work alone and requests a checklist so he can "cover it all" solo (T73, sess_d40c8827) — a reasonable scaffold in isolation, but in service of avoiding the collaboration itself, which recurs as a pattern (see below).

The less reassuring instance is a World Literature session where he admits he hadn't done the reading and asks the tutor to substitute for it: "some short story packet, i didnt really read it" (T59, sess_2a90c113). The tutor declined without the actual text — and rather than doing the reading, he disengaged entirely: "nvm ill just deal with it tomorrow." Unlike the Social Studies session two weeks later, where a similar opening ("can you just summarize what causes of the depression they probably want") turned out to have real material behind it and became genuine Conceptual work once he was asked to check his own reasoning, this one produced nothing in either direction.

## What's missing

Critical Thinking and Creative work are both real but confined to single strong sessions rather than a habit — the capability is proven, particularly in English, but it doesn't show up reliably. That's different from Transfer, which is rare by nature in most students; here it's rare but unusually consequential each time, which suggests the gap is occasion, not ability.

The more interesting gap is behavioral rather than cognitive: a documented pattern of withdrawing from a task rather than attempting it, concentrated almost entirely in December through February. Two sessions (World Literature, Studio Art) end in the student simply disengaging without redoing the work, and a third (the Computer Science pairing) shows him actively avoiding a collaborative assignment rather than a cognitively demanding one. By April, that same collaboration pattern reverses completely — he pairs with a partner, catches a real bug in the partner's code, and works through the honest way to raise it (T99–T103, sess_4d612bf1). The withdrawal was real, and so is its end.

## For the teacher

**Name the connection he already made himself.** In February he connected the December transfer to his exam performance out loud, unprompted, then called it something he "didn't really think about... like a thing that would show up on a test." A direct, low-stakes conversation confirming that connection — not delivering it as news — would meet him where he already is.

**Use physics and robotics as the on-ramp back into everything else.** His strongest, most independent thinking all year clusters there specifically, and his engagement and sense of belonging visibly recover together starting in March. The April demonstration and the May robotics-club question are both real, self-initiated openings.

**In English, ask for the real difference, not just that a comparison exists — and keep pairing him for group work.** His comparative thesis work is genuinely strong once he engages (T25, T117), and session 13 shows he can collaborate well now that he's chosen to.

**Follow up this week on the robotics club, not next fall.** He already named a plausible sponsor (Ms. Chen) and a specific reason he waited five months to ask. The motivation is dated; the window to use it is now.

## Caveats

This is one AI tutor across one school year and doesn't capture in-class work, paper homework, tests, robotics or club time, or conversations with teachers and peers. The sample is skewed toward homework-shaped and troubleshooting sessions — that's when he opens the tool — which likely undercounts whatever he does easily without help, especially in physics. The three earliest sessions reflect a different school's curriculum and pacing before the December transfer, so comparing pre- and post-transfer sessions directly should account for unfamiliar content at the new school, not only the disruption itself. Depth ratings reflect what he wrote in the moment, not independently verified understanding or retention.
