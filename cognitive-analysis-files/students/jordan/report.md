# Cognitive Task Analysis: Student Jordan Whitaker (Grade 10) - School Year 2025-2026

## Snapshot

Across 16 sessions and nine months, Jordan's clearest and most consistent move is **inventing a strategy under pressure and then reusing it, unprompted, in a completely different subject**. It starts small - three boxes before a timed essay so an idea doesn't disappear on the page - and by January they're explicitly recognizing "thats three times ive done this now" as they build the same kind of strategy for a math test. By the final review session in May they trace the whole pattern back to a sculpture class in November. The single most important thing to know: their depth tracks task *format*, not subject or difficulty. When a task lets them build, diagram, or talk something through, they do sustained, often independent conceptual and creative work. When a task is shaped like "produce correct writing, alone, under a clock," the same knowledge is there but the format is actively working against them - and by the end of the year, they've started building their own accommodations for that gap rather than waiting for the format to change.

**Evidence base:** 120 student turns across 16 sessions spanning September 2025 to May 2026, across five subject areas. This is enough breadth and duration to see a strategy go from tutor-scaffolded to fully independent.

## What the evidence shows

### Metacognitive - precise self-diagnosis that becomes a strategy

This is the most frequent type in the transcript, and it's unusually specific. Before a quadratics test in January, Jordan didn't just say they were anxious - they located the exact mechanism: "quadratics, all of unit 4. i can do every problem type if you just talk to me or if i have a whiteboard, but a written test with a clock i just blank" (T121, sess_j08). That's not a complaint, it's a diagnosis precise enough to act on, and the tutor pointed out they'd already solved an analogous problem for a different subject. Jordan built a four-line method list, timed themselves against three practice problems, and closed the session by noticing the pattern themselves: "yeah i guess thats three times ive done this now" (T135).

By the final exam review in May, that noticing had become explicit self-knowledge: "been doing the method list for other subjects all year without even really deciding to" (T218, sess_j16), traced immediately back to a specific sculpture session: "i think in art actually, when i said the skeleton thing was the same problem as november. i didnt plan to start doing that everywhere i just kept doing it bc it worked the first time" (T220).

A smaller but equally telling instance: mid-solve on a semester exam problem, Jordan noticed their own answer matched a different problem's answer and stopped to check rather than moving on - "wait thats the same as problem 2 thats weird" (T100, sess_j06) - and traced it to a real copying error two turns later.

### Transfer - rare in most transcripts, already a habit in this one

Most cognitive-task analyses report Transfer as the thinnest category: proven but occasional. Here it shows up in 6 of 16 sessions, and every instance is fully self-initiated. In November, reading a history assignment, Jordan noticed mid-session that their own reading strategy matched their essay-writing strategy from a different class weeks earlier: "like for the essay i made the three boxes before writing sentences, and for this i made the three headings mean something before reading paragraphs. its the same move just for reading instead of writing" (T71, sess_j04). In December, designing a game for a computer science project, they recognized their own speed formula shared its structure with a compound-interest formula from math class, then caught themselves on a real distinction: "wait is it different bc mine is linear and thats compound which is multiplying" (T114, sess_j07) - correctly resolving that the game needed exponential, not linear, growth. In April, designing a sculpture, they recognized a structural problem as one they'd already solved five months earlier: "oh yeah i need an actual skeleton underneath again, i keep forgetting thats always the answer" (T193, sess_j13).

### Conceptual - the clearest single strength

In the very first session on record, asked to explain systems of equations, Jordan requested a physical analogy, was given one, and then - after the tutor declined to keep supplying them - built their own for a harder problem unprompted: "i picture like. one side is heavy with x's and the other has to balance with less y. if i add them the y cancels bc -y and +y" (T17, sess_j01). The clearest instance of the year is a computer science session on recursion, where Jordan reasoned out the actual mechanism from a guess before being shown it: "then every call would be changing the same variables as every other call so itd probably just overwrite everything and give a wrong answer, or maybe infinite loop bc the base case variable never actually gets its own separate value" (T164, sess_j11) - which is, correctly, the actual reason recursive calls need their own stack frame.

### Critical Thinking and Creative - strong whenever the task asks for a position or a direction

Literature and history sessions show a repeated arc: Jordan states a real thesis, is asked for evidence, and by the final essay of the year does the whole sequence without scaffolding - "topic is free choice... box 1: both characters hide the truth. box 2: gregor's hiding is selfless... box 3: odysseus's hiding is self interested even when he frames it as protecting others" (T210, sess_j15), then defends a doubt about their own claim rather than backing down from it: "i think its fair bc even when he says its to protect his crew he still ends up being the one who survives and gets credit" (T214). On the research project, asked to choose ten events for a timeline, Jordan picked their own and justified the one they cut on a stated criterion - "i left out the panama canal bc it didnt really cause anything later it was just a thing that happened" (T157, sess_j10) - real evaluative judgment, not acceptance of a supplied list.

Creative work is strongest exactly where the student supplies the direction: an original visual metaphor for a personal theme, developed before any material was chosen (T185, sess_j13), and a spontaneous, quotable thesis line that arrived mid-sentence rather than on request: oh like open with "the odyssey doesnt punish lying it punishes losing" or something (T144, sess_j09).

## Where thinking was offloaded

Five instances, and none of them show a full assignment handed over and returned unexamined. In September, Jordan asked the tutor to keep generating physical analogies instead of building their own; the tutor declined, and Jordan produced a correct original analogy two turns later (T15-T17, sess_j01). In October, asked the tutor to write an essay outline directly; declined, and Jordan built their own three-part outline immediately after (T49-T51, sess_j03). In December, asked for worked answers to a whole practice set; the tutor worked one problem and required Jordan to do the rest, which surfaced a real copying error Jordan caught and fixed themselves (T94-T102, sess_j06). In February, asked for a ready-made list of ten historical events; declined, and Jordan chose their own and justified a cut (T155-T157, sess_j10). The one offload that was simply granted was trivial: reformatting a diagram Jordan had already reasoned out completely themselves (T168, sess_j11) - a legitimate delegation, since no cognitive work was left in it.

## What's missing

Fluency and Exploratory are the thinnest categories, and for the same underlying reason: most of this transcript is assignment-shaped, so there's little room for either open-ended browsing or deliberate drilling for its own sake. That's a reasonable feature of a transcript pulled from homework help, not a gap worth acting on by itself.

The one place a gap looks more like an unexamined shortcut than an absence of opportunity: the rational-expressions session, where Jordan asked to skip explanation entirely - "dont ask me to explain it i just want practice" - and then admitted, when asked directly, "honestly i dont fully get why you flip the fraction when you divide, i just remember to do it" (T178-T180, sess_j12). That's real fluency at the level of execution sitting on top of a conceptual gap that hasn't been checked. It's the only place in the transcript where "I've got this" and "I understand this" come apart.

## For the teacher

**Let Jordan use their own method-first list as a standing accommodation on timed tests, not something to reinvent each time.** This transcript shows a large, repeated gap between what they can do live or hands-on and what shows up on a timed written page - and shows them closing that exact gap themselves once they had a way to decide the method before starting to solve (T119-T135, sess_j08). Making that step official protects a fix that's already proven to work.

**Ask, roughly monthly, where else a strategy that worked once has shown up.** Jordan independently reused the same self-built strategy in at least four different subjects without being told to generalize it, and named the pattern themselves twice (T71, T135, T220). The habit is already strong; naming it out loud with an adult would make it more deliberate rather than something that happens to keep working.

**Treat "can I do this differently" as self-advocacy, not a loophole.** The WWI essay session shows Jordan asking to swap a written assignment for a physical model, accepting a real explanation of why the assignment specifically wants writing, and negotiating a genuinely useful compromise - sketching the causal chain as prewriting instead of a standard outline (T194-T207, sess_j14). That's the model worth using generally: the alternate format is for finding the thesis, the assignment's format is for stating it.

**When Jordan says they "just remember" a procedure, spend two minutes on why it works before more practice.** The rational-expressions session shows a real gap hiding behind fluent execution. That's exactly the kind of gap that survives homework and breaks on an unfamiliar problem.

## Caveats

This is one AI tutor on one platform across one school year - it doesn't capture class time, paper homework, tests, or conversations with teachers and peers, which make up most of Jordan's actual academic day. The sessions here are self-selected: Jordan brought these conversations to the tutor, usually because something was due or they were stuck, which skews the sample toward homework-shaped tasks and away from whatever they already do easily without help. Depth ratings reflect what appears in the chat text, not independently verified retention or in-class performance. This transcript and analysis were authored for a product demonstration and do not represent a real student.
