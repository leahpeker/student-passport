# Cognitive Task Analysis: Maya Okonkwo — Grade 10 — School Year 2025–2026

## Snapshot

Across 17 sessions and nine months, Maya's dominant move is narrating her own thinking — she catches herself mid-process more often and more precisely than any other pattern in this transcript, from diagnosing a failed study method to noticing that her own self-criticism in class might be a performance rather than a genuine reaction to her work. Her second-clearest strength is repairing broken mental models: she names a wrong idea in her own words and rebuilds it, most visibly with logarithms in March. The single most important thing to know: almost none of her weakest moments are about ability. When she offloads, it's arithmetic or a final verification pass, never the core reasoning. What actually costs her something is a pattern that isn't captured well by "offloading" at all — she frequently hands the tutor a request for a verdict ("is a 94 bad," "is this an A thesis") on work she's already finished, as if she needs permission to stop. Twice, the tutor declined to just answer and asked what she thought instead, and both times that refusal produced her sharpest thinking of the session.

**Evidence base:** 100 student turns across 17 sessions spanning September 2025 to May 2026, across six subject areas. This is enough breadth and duration to support a genuine profile, including which patterns are habitual versus one-off.

## What the evidence shows

### Metacognitive — constant, and occasionally very sharp

This is the most frequent type in the transcript, and it runs from routine to genuinely striking. In November, ahead of a history exam, she diagnosed her own study method rather than asking to be re-taught: "reading it and highlighting stuff I already knew I guess" (T36, sess_m04g8h9i), then after trying active recall instead: "ok wait that's uncomfortable, I got like half of it just from memory and had to check the rest" (T38).

The sharpest instance in the corpus came after a strong grade she'd publicly dismissed. Looking at a 94 she'd called "the worst thing I'd turned in," she caught herself: "it's not the grade i guess. i said out loud in class it was the worst thing i'd turned in and then i got a 94 on it and now i don't know if i actually think that or if i was just performing being hard on myself" (T121, sess_m11u2v3w). A month later, revising an essay paragraph for the sixth time, she produced the most precise self-diagnosis in the transcript: "i think the real answer is i'm not actually editing anymore, i'm just checking" (T139, sess_m13y6z7a) — and then set her own rule for when to stop: "two more read throughs and then i'm done regardless of how i feel about it" (T141).

### Conceptual — repairing broken models

Her clearest strength when a task invites building rather than applying. In a March session on logarithms, she opened with a genuine misconception — "i keep thinking of it as how many times 2 goes into 8 which is 4 not 3" (T128, sess_m12w4x5y) — and replaced it in real time: "OH. it's not division at all, it's asking what power. so log is literally just 'the exponent' dressed up" (T130). In a January session introducing recursion, she extended a new concept to a boundary case unprompted: "wait so for something like a file system with folders inside folders a loop wouldn't even really work the same way right, because you don't know how deep it goes" (T77, sess_m07m4n5o).

### Critical Thinking — strong on evaluation, and reused on purpose

Notably strong whenever a task asks her to weigh sources against each other, and notable for showing up twice with the same underlying method. In January, working practice DBQ documents, she built a distinction between a government memo and a propaganda newspaper: "I think I'd use the memo for what actually happened and the newspaper only for what the Soviets wanted people to believe, not as a fact source" (T90, sess_m08o6p7q). In April, on the real assignment, she built a more sophisticated, synthesized thesis using the same method: "my thesis is going to be that personal accounts show the human cost even when the official numbers look good, so you need both, not that one is right" (T150, sess_m14a8b9c) — and named the reuse herself: "yeah I remembered that conversation actually. glad i didn't have to relearn it" (T152).

### Transfer — rare, but the single most interesting turn in the transcript

That same April turn extends past Critical Thinking into a genuine Transfer instance: she connects the historical-source method to a completely different context — the tutor itself. "it's kind of the same thing as when I don't just believe whatever you tell me either, right, same idea but with documents instead of you" (T152). This is the only Transfer instance in the corpus, but it's self-initiated, unprompted by the tutor, and applies a reasoning principle across a genuinely different kind of source. One instance in seventeen sessions is a small sample, but it's fully realized — proof of capability, not a fluke.

### Creative — thin, but self-directed when it shows up

Twice in the corpus she generates her own standard rather than following an assignment's. In a studio session on a self-portrait, she diagnosed her own error precisely: "i think i was trying to fix a feeling instead of a measurement" (T16, sess_m02c4d5e). In May, working her final exhibition piece, she rejected a technically correct composition for feeling "too safe" and chose a riskier one instead: "I want to try something asymmetrical instead of centering it, even though centering is safer" (T169, sess_m16e2f3g), later noting the piece felt more honestly hers: "this is actually better and also terrifying because now I can't blame the assignment if it doesn't work" (T173).

### Procedural and Fluency — reliable, and where the real offloading lives

Procedural work is her most consistent category by volume: debugging her own code from a guiding question ("oh wait. I never actually initialized current before the loop," T53, sess_m05i0j1k) and independently solving an unfamiliar exponential equation before checking it by estimation (T158–T160, sess_m15c0d1e). This is also where the transcript's actual task-offloading lives, though it's narrow: a final verification pass after she'd already done the real work (T9, T59), and one outright handoff of a grade-average calculation at 1am (T66, sess_m06k2l3m) and afterward she asked "why does that not make me feel better" (T70).

## Where thinking was offloaded

Three real offloading instances, and all three are low-stakes: a final proof check after she'd already rewritten it correctly herself (T9), a request for a broader code review after fixing two bugs on her own (T60, which actually produced a useful edge-case catch she then reasoned through herself), and a one-off grade-average calculation handed over outright (T66).

The more interesting pattern in this transcript isn't task offloading at all — it's verdict-seeking. Twice she asked the tutor to simply judge her finished work rather than engage with it: "can you just tell me honestly if it's an A thesis or a B thesis" (T31, sess_m03e6f7g) and "Is a 94 bad. Be honest." (T119, sess_m11u2v3w). Both times, the tutor declined to just answer. In the English session, it also declined a direct request to rewrite a weak paragraph for her ("can you fix that paragraph," T101, sess_m09q8r9s) and asked what she thought was missing instead — and she found it herself: "I guess I never said why the second example matters, I just listed it" (T103). In every one of these cases, the refusal is what produced the thinking, not the request.

## What's missing

Exploratory and Transfer are the thinnest categories, for different reasons. Transfer is thin because the habit hasn't formed, not because she's incapable — the one instance is strong and self-initiated, so the gap is frequency, not ability. Exploratory is thin because she rarely stays in intake mode: the moment she encounters something new, she tends to move into testing it, building on it, or applying it, which is a reasonable and even a good sign, but it means pure open-ended curiosity barely appears in this transcript.

The one place a gap looks more like a cost than an absence of opportunity is the reassurance-seeking pattern. It isn't offloading in the usual sense — no task gets handed over — but it is a real drain: several of her latest-night sessions (T31, T64–72, T95–97, T119–121) produce little cognitive content because the actual request is for permission to stop worrying, not for help thinking. The tutor's refusal to just supply that verdict is doing real work here, and it's worth knowing that the pattern would look very different with a tool that simply answered.

## For the teacher

**When she asks for a yes/no verdict, ask what she thinks first.** "Is a 94 bad," "is this good enough," "is this an A thesis" — every time the tutor declined to just answer and asked her opinion instead, she produced real thinking (T121–123 is the sharpest example in the whole transcript). This is a low-cost, high-yield move any adult in her life can make.

**Prompt Transfer on purpose, roughly monthly.** She connected a source-evaluation method across two assignments months apart, and extended it to a third context (trusting the tutor itself) completely unprompted. That's a proven capability that hasn't become a habit — ask her directly where else a method from one class shows up in another.

**Redirect grade-math questions away from the tutor.** "What does a 95 do to my average" is arithmetic, not learning, and by her own account it doesn't touch the underlying worry. A syllabus grade calculator serves the same purpose without costing her a 1am conversation.

**Name the Creative pattern to her directly.** She rejected a safe, correct choice for a riskier, more original one in her final art piece and seemed almost surprised by her own decision. Given how much of her other work is graded on correctness, she may not recognize this instinct as a strength unless someone points it out.

## Caveats

This is one tool on one platform across one school year — it doesn't capture in-class work, paper homework, tests, or conversations with teachers and peers, which make up most of her actual academic life. The sessions here are self-selected: she brought these conversations to an AI tutor, usually late at night and usually because something was due or she was anxious about it, which skews the sample toward homework-adjacent and worry-adjacent moments and away from whatever she already does easily. Depth ratings reflect what she wrote in the moment, not independently verified retention. All data in this analysis is synthetic, generated for a product demonstration.
