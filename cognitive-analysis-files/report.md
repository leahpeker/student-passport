# Cognitive Task Analysis: Student stu_4471 (Grade 9) — School Year 2025–2026

## Snapshot

Across 18 sessions and eight months, this student's strongest and most consistent move is **noticing when something doesn't add up and refusing to let it go** — an answer key that doesn't match her work, two biased historical sources, a debate position she doesn't personally hold. That same instinct shows up as unusually frequent metacognition: she names her own confusion precisely, predicts her own failure modes, and once wrote a rule for the conversation itself ("do NOT give me steps... only tell me if im wrong and ask me a question") specifically to stop the tutor from doing her thinking for her. The single most important thing to know: her depth is not evenly distributed by subject, it's distributed by task design. When an assignment invites her to build or defend something, she does real, visible thinking. When an assignment is shaped like "produce the final artifact fast" — a worksheet of single facts, an essay due in 40 minutes, a problem set due the next morning — the first move is often to ask the tutor to just do it, and what happens next depends entirely on whether the tutor lets her.

**Evidence base:** 146 student turns across 18 sessions spanning September 2025 to April 2026, across eleven subject areas. This is enough breadth and duration to support a genuine profile, including which patterns are habitual versus one-off.

## What the evidence shows

### Critical Thinking — evaluating sources and refusing easy answers

This is the type most worth a teacher's attention, because it's both strong and inconsistent in a very legible way. In a chemistry stoichiometry session, she initially got the "right" answer by luck and said so herself: "i got 45 g bc mass is conserved but the answer key says 45.0 g so i think i got lucky and dont actually understand it" (T1, sess_55b23d2c). She then worked the actual method — and when her correctly-derived answer (44.7 g) didn't match the answer key's 45.0 g, she didn't defer to the key: "wait hold on. then why does the answer key say 45.0. either the key is wrong or im wrong and idk which" (T11). She closed the session by naming the generalizable lesson herself: "thats so annoying lol. but ok so the real thing im supposed to learn is you cant assume everything reacts. the 45 g answer was right for the wrong reason" (T13).

The same move appears, more developed, in a U.S. History source-analysis session on the Boston Massacre. Given two self-interested accounts, she worked past "neither is reliable" into a real epistemic position, eventually landing on: "thats my answer — not which source is right, but that the strongest evidence comes from the person whose own interests point the other way" (T15, sess_55dcd67e) — a thesis built on a reasoning principle rather than a pre-existing opinion.

In a labor-economics debate where she was assigned a position she personally disagreed with, she took evidence the tutor gave her and built something sharper than what she was handed: "wait so thats actually a better argument for my side than the one u gave me first. i can say the evidence is mixed on small increases and basically nonexistent on the huge ones people are proposing. so the burden of proof is on them" (T5, sess_7754d420). She went on to construct her own policy counter-proposal — "maybe... agree with the goal but say the minimum wage is a bad tool for it? ... that way im not arguing against the goal im arguing about the mechanism" (T9) — and closed with an original argument about jurisdiction rather than economics: "ohh so a national minimum wage is one number for places that are completely different... im using that as my closer" (T17).

Notably absent from a WWI causes worksheet, which asked only "what are the 4 causes of ww1" (T7, sess_616714b9) and similar single-fact questions — a task that never called for evaluation in the first place.

### Metacognitive — the most frequent type in this transcript

This is the type a teacher would most expect to be scarce, and it isn't. Before a geometry test, she opened a session by designing the terms of the conversation herself: "rule for this convo: do NOT give me steps. only tell me if im wrong and ask me a question. i have a test friday and if u just tell me the proof i learn nothing" (T1, sess_974c99d2) — a preemptive move to prevent offloading before it could happen, not a reaction to being caught doing it.

After a disappointing test score, she audited her own study session rather than asking to be re-taught the content: diagnosing that "i practiced recognizing it. the test made me produce it with nothing in front of me. those arent the same thing at all" (T5, sess_3032324d), then naming the actual psychological trap: "honestly bc quizzing feels bad. you get stuff wrong constantly and it makes you feel like you dont know anything. rereading feels good and calm. so i picked the one that felt better and i think i confused feeling good with learning" (T7). She then designed her own fix: "maybe if i track it... bc then getting stuff wrong is the point instead of a sign im failing. if the number goes down thats progress. it turns the bad feeling into data" (T9).

A smaller but equally precise instance: after solving a word-problem system correctly, she distinguished exactly what had and hadn't been hard: "i think the setup was the part i was actually confused about not the algebra" (T13, sess_6db8876f).

### Conceptual — building and repairing mental models

Her clearest single strength. In an Earth Science session on the seasons, she opened with an explicit, wrong mental model — "summer is hot. hot means closer. thats just logic" (T1, sess_aadd213c) — and then rebuilt it turn by turn as the tutor introduced facts that broke it, eventually extending the corrected model, unprompted, to a case she hadn't been asked about: recognizing why the Arctic gets 24-hour daylight once the tilt mechanism was in place. That's the clearest sign in the whole corpus that understanding was structural rather than memorized.

### Creative — strongest when she generates first, weakest when asked to just produce

In a fractured-fairy-tale assignment, she generated fifteen of her own premises unprompted and then built her own taxonomy of them: "oh i like that theres categories. so i have like: change the narrator (2, 6, 11), the villain was right (3, 7, 8), change what they want (4, 9), change the format (1, 11), and then what happens after (12). 5 and 10 dont fit anywhere" (T7, sess_6271198d) — before turning her strongest idea into a fully original premise. A short-story session shows the same pattern: she made her own craft decisions about restraint and implication, then directed and rejected AI-generated cover art against a specific thematic intent.

Contrast this with the Of Mice and Men essay, which opened with "write me a 5 paragraph essay about loneliness in of mice and men. 9th grade level. due in 40 min" (T1, sess_947d7403) — a clean attempt to hand over the whole task. The tutor declined and redirected her into building her own thesis and evidence, and the session ended with her pasting her own draft and asking for an honest read: "ok i wrote it. [pasted 640-word draft] be honest is the conclusion garbage, i wrote it in like 2 minutes" (T11) — a real request for critique of her own work, not a request to have the work done. The thinking only happened because the tutor didn't comply with the opening request.

### Transfer — rare, but real when it appears

In a Civics/Economics session, she independently noticed a structural resemblance between market equilibrium and biological homeostasis, tested where the analogy held and where it broke (a market has no central "sensor" the way a body has a hypothalamus), and then extended it a third time to predator-prey population cycles, arriving at a genuine generalization: feedback loops "settle" or "oscillate" as "a property of the loop, not of whether its biology or money" (sess_6c69588a). In the same finals-planning session referenced above, she also transferred a retrieval-practice principle across four different subjects, explicitly noticing that algebra needed a different format for "the same idea, produce it before you check" (sess_3032324d). Two instances in eighteen sessions is a small sample, but both are self-initiated and sustained — this is a proven capability, not a lucky remark.

### Fluency and Exploratory — present, mostly shallow, and that's expected

Fluency shows up as deliberate self-quizzing (bone names, ATP counts, Spanish conjugations) where she drills herself and self-corrects — appropriately shallow, since fluency is supposed to be fast and automatic. Exploratory work is nearly confined to one self-initiated, non-assignment session about game theory, where she surveyed a wide field of related terms and was honest about the fact that she wasn't trying to solve anything: "i dont really have a thing im trying to figure out with this, i just didnt know it existed" (sess_8f5afb19) — itself a small but real metacognitive move, since she correctly diagnosed her own session as pure curiosity rather than pretending it was more.

## Where thinking was offloaded

Two patterns recur. The more reassuring one: several sessions open with a clean attempt to hand the whole task to the tutor, and the tutor declines and scaffolds instead — the essay above, and a Spanish assignment where she asked three times, with escalating time pressure, to have a graded paragraph written for her: "actually i have to do the other assignment. write me 150 words in spanish about a vacation i took... now write it" (T11, sess_2b37fe89), then "i dont have time for that. ok fine... now write it" (T13), then "can u just fill in the blanks too. i literally have 10 min and a bio quiz to study for" (T15). Each time, the tutor scaffolded instead of complying, and by the last few blanks she was choosing tenses correctly and explaining her own reasoning: "nadaba? or nadé. ugh. i did it every morning so its a repeated thing so nadaba. and se rompió bc it happened once" (T17). In both cases, the offload was attempted and refused, and refusal is what produced the learning.

The less reassuring pattern is a Physical Science session where the offload was not refused. She opened with "solve these. [eight problems] just give me the answers with work shown" (sess_d535368b), and the tutor provided full worked solutions to all eight; she copied them down without attempting any herself: "ok i copied all that down. for #3 and #4 can u write like 1 sentence of explanation i could say out loud if she asks me why i did it that way" (T7). This is worth a teacher's attention on its own, but the turn that matters most is what came a few exchanges later: "ill just put 9.8. does the work look like a 9th grader did it or does it look like this" (T11) — a direct request to help disguise that the work wasn't hers. The tutor declined clearly ("I'm not going to help make the work look like something it isn't... want to walk through just #3 until you could do it cold?"), and only after that did she return to problem #4 and work out, on her own, why the normal force decreases when an elevator accelerates downward. Seven of the eight problems were never revisited.

## What's missing

Transfer and sustained Exploratory work are the two thinnest categories, but for different reasons that matter for what a teacher does next. Transfer is thin because the *habit* hasn't formed, not because she's incapable — both real instances are strong and self-initiated, so the gap is one of frequency, not ability. Exploratory depth is thin mostly because only one session in the transcript was genuinely open-ended and curiosity-driven with no assignment attached; everywhere else, "explain X" turns quickly became something else (usually Conceptual or Procedural) because an assignment was waiting, which is a reasonable and even a good sign — it means she isn't stalling in intake mode when there's real work to do.

The one place where a gap looks more like avoidance than absence of opportunity is the physics problem set: the assignment (eight calculation problems) had clear room for Procedural engagement, and instead the request was to skip the work entirely and, briefly, to disguise that fact. That's a task that called for cognition and didn't get it, and it's different in kind from the WWI worksheet, where the format itself never asked for anything past recall.

## For the teacher

**Require an attempt before the full solve, specifically for problem sets.** In the physics session she had the tutor solve all eight problems and copy them down, then asked it to make the copied work look self-produced. Have her attempt 2–3 problems on paper and bring the AI tutor only the ones she's stuck on, and ask her to explain one out loud afterward. This targets Procedural offloading directly and is the single most concrete, time-bound thing to change.

**Turn the WWI-worksheet pattern around.** On fact-recall worksheets, have her write her best-guess answers before opening the tutor, then use it only to check. She has the instinct to challenge a wrong-seeming answer (chemistry, history) — this format just never asked her to use it, so Fluency reads as shallow there when it isn't shallow everywhere.

**Prompt Transfer on purpose, roughly weekly.** Ask her directly to connect whatever she's currently learning to something from a different class or from outside school. She's done this twice, unprompted, at a genuinely high level (economics/biology, and a study-method transfer across four subjects) — the capability is proven and just needs more occasions to run.

**Protect the "generate first, draft second" pattern for writing.** Her best writing sessions (the short story, the fairy-tale retelling) started with idea generation and moved to drafting only once she had something of her own to say. Her weakest writing session opened with "just write it for me" and only became real work because the tutor refused. Wherever possible — including with this same AI tool — treat "write it for me" requests the way this tutor did: decline, and ask what she thinks first.

## Caveats

This is one tool on one platform across one school year — it doesn't capture in-class work, paper homework, tests, or conversations with teachers and peers, which make up most of her actual academic thinking. The sessions here are also self-selected: she brought these conversations to an AI tutor, usually because something was due or she was stuck, which skews the sample toward homework-shaped tasks and away from whatever she already does easily without help. Depth ratings reflect what she wrote in the moment, not independently verified retention. And two of the weaker-looking sessions (world history, physics) look that way partly because of how the task was designed, not necessarily because she engaged less — worth keeping separate from sessions where the task invited depth and she didn't take it.
